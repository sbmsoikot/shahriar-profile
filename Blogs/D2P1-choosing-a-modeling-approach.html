Domain 2 (ML Model Development, 26%) opens with Task 2.1: choose a modeling approach. Before anyone tunes a hyperparameter, you decide what kind of model this even is — a pre-built AWS service, an AWS-provided algorithm you train, or something custom. This post is that decision, made concrete on MetroPulse.

Previously on MetroPulse Across Domain 1 we built the data foundation for MetroPulse, a city-scale mobility platform. Part 1 set up ingestion & storage — Kinesis → Managed Service for Apache Flink → Amazon Data Firehose into an S3 Bronze/Silver/Gold lake. Part 2 turned that raw data into model-ready features — EMR/Spark and Glue transforms, the cleaning gauntlet, TF-IDF on support tickets, Ground Truth labeling of damage photos, a Feature Store, and Athena-curated tables. The data is prepared. Now we choose how to model it.

01 / THE FORKThe modeling spectrum — and why the project doesn't need to change

Quick honesty check first, the same one I did before each previous post: does MetroPulse still fit the syllabus? This time the answer is an emphatic yes, and then some. The two data facets I added in Part 2 — support-ticket text and damage photos — were partly a bet on this exact domain. They pay off now: they unlock most of the managed AI services (Comprehend, Translate, Transcribe, Lex, Rekognition, Textract) and several built-in algorithms (BlazingText, Object Detection, topic models). Combined with the original telemetry and payments data, MetroPulse can legitimately exercise nearly every modeling option AWS offers. No project change needed — the groundwork was laid deliberately.

Task 2.1 is really about placing each business problem on a spectrum. At one end you buy: call a fully-managed AI service and let AWS's pre-trained model do the work. In the middle you configure: bring your own data to a SageMaker built-in algorithm — proven, optimized, no model code required. At the far end you build: write custom training code, or customize a foundation model. As you move right, you gain control and customization but take on more effort, cost, and ML expertise.

The brief · Domain 2, Part 1

One platform, many model decisions

MetroPulse has a dozen problems that each want a model. The job for this post isn't to train them — it's to place each one on the spectrum and justify the call:

  • Ticket sentiment & PII, transcription, translation, chatbot → almost certainly buy (these are solved problems).
  • Demand forecasting, predictive maintenance, anomaly detection, segmentation, fraud → likely configure with a built-in algorithm on our own data.
  • Anything genuinely novel or needing deep customization → build (Part 2's custom training, or the GenAI post's foundation models).

The golden rule, baked into Fig 1: pick the leftmost option that actually solves the problem. Don't train a model when an API will do; don't call a generic API when your data demands a tailored one.

Exam lens Task 2.1 questions almost always describe a business problem and ask for the most appropriate (often "least operational overhead" or "fastest to deploy") approach. The trap is over-engineering: if Amazon Comprehend already does sentiment analysis, "train a custom model" is the wrong answer even though it would work. Read for the constraint — speed, cost, expertise, or customization — and let it point you along the spectrum.

02 / WHY BUYThe case for managed AI services

For a huge class of problems — recognizing objects in a photo, transcribing speech, detecting sentiment — the model has already been built, trained on more data than MetroPulse will ever have, and exposed as a simple API. AWS managed AI services let you consume that intelligence with a function call: no training, no infrastructure, no ML PhD. You pay per request, AWS keeps improving the model underneath, and it scales automatically.

The tradeoff is control. A managed service does what it does; you can't reshape its internals. For genuinely domain-specific needs, several services offer a middle path — Comprehend custom classification, Rekognition Custom Labels, Transcribe custom vocabulary — where you supply a little of your own data to specialize a managed model without building one from scratch. But the default instinct should be: if a managed service solves it, use it.

Callback Notice A2I and Ground Truth (Part 2) both route work to human reviewers — but at different moments. Ground Truth labels a dataset before training; A2I reviews live, low-confidence predictions after deployment. Same workforces, different stage of the lifecycle.

05 / Q & SILICONAmazon Q and the AI hardware underneath

Two more pieces round out the "buy" tier. Amazon Q is AWS's family of generative-AI assistants. Q Business connects to your enterprise systems and answers questions over your own content — MetroPulse could point it at ops runbooks, policies, and dashboards so staff get grounded answers instead of hunting through wikis. Q Apps lets non-developers turn a prompt into a lightweight internal app. Q Developer is the AI coding assistant (the successor to CodeWhisperer) that lives in the IDE, CLI, and Console. One thing worth knowing as of 2026: AWS is shifting Q Developer — new sign-ups are being curtailed and its newest coding models are moving to AWS's Kiro experience — so treat the conceptual role (an AWS-aware coding assistant) as the durable, exam-relevant idea rather than the exact packaging.

Underneath every training and inference job sits silicon, and AWS builds its own. AWS Trainium chips (now through Trainium2 and Trainium3) are purpose-built for training at lower cost than comparable GPU instances; AWS Inferentia chips (through Inferentia2) are optimized for inference — high throughput, low cost per prediction. You target both through the AWS Neuron SDK. For MetroPulse, the practical takeaway is cost: when our models graduate to heavy, steady workloads, Trainium/Inferentia instances can cut the bill substantially versus general-purpose GPUs.

Exam lens Keep the chip split crisp: Trainium = training, Inferentia = inference, both accessed via the Neuron SDK, both chosen for price-performance over GPUs. If a question stresses "reduce training cost" → Trainium; "reduce inference cost at scale" → Inferentia.

06 / CONFIGUREWhen you need your own model: SageMaker built-in algorithms

Managed services are perfect until your problem is yours. No API knows what "normal" battery drain looks like for MetroPulse scooters in winter, or how this city's demand curves bend on game nights. For those, you need a model trained on your data — but you almost never need to implement the algorithm yourself. SageMaker AI (the renamed core ML platform from Part 2) ships a catalog of built-in algorithms: AWS-implemented, performance-tuned, distributed-by-default versions of the workhorse ML algorithms. You bring data to S3, pick the algorithm, set a few hyperparameters, and launch a training job. No model code.

SageMaker input modes — how data reaches the training job

One small but testable detail: how the algorithm reads data from S3. File mode (the default) copies the entire dataset onto the training instance's disk before starting — simple, fine for datasets that fit. Pipe mode streams data directly from S3 as the job runs — lower startup time and no need to fit the data on disk, good for very large datasets. FastFile mode is the modern best-of-both: data is streamed on demand but accessed as if it were local files, so you get Pipe-like scalability with File-like simplicity. Built-in algorithms also favor efficient formats like RecordIO-protobuf alongside CSV.

Common trap "The training dataset is hundreds of GB and won't fit on the instance volume." That's a Pipe mode (or FastFile mode) signal — stream from S3 rather than downloading. Don't reach for a bigger, pricier instance just to hold the whole file when streaming solves it.

09 / DECIDEBuy vs configure vs build: the framework

Strip away the service names and the decision comes down to a few questions. Ask them in order and the answer usually falls out.

First: is this a common, solved problem? Sentiment, transcription, translation, object recognition, search — if yes, buy. Second: does a managed service exist but not quite fit your data or accuracy bar? Try its customization feature (Comprehend custom, Rekognition Custom Labels) before escalating. Third: is the problem specific to your data but a standard ML shape (tabular prediction, forecasting, clustering, anomaly detection)? Then configure a built-in algorithm. Only when the problem is genuinely novel, needs full architectural control, or calls for generative capability do you build — custom training (Part 2) or a foundation model (the GenAI post).

If the scenario stresses…Lean toward…
"Least operational overhead" / "fastest to deploy" / "no ML expertise"Buy — managed AI service
"Common task" (sentiment, OCR, speech, objects, search)Buy
"Managed service, but our domain terms / categories"Buy + customization feature
"Train on our own data" + a standard ML shapeConfigure — built-in algorithm
"Full control of the model" / novel architectureBuild — custom training
"Generate text / summarize / reason / chat"Build — foundation model (Bedrock)
The two classic traps Over-engineering: choosing "train a custom model" when a managed service already does the job — wrong whenever the prompt rewards speed or low overhead. Under-powering: forcing a generic API onto a problem that genuinely needs your data — a managed sentiment API won't learn MetroPulse's domain jargon the way a custom classifier will. The spectrum exists precisely so you don't default to either extreme.

Domain 2, Part 1 revision cheat-sheet

  • Task 2.1 = choose a modeling approach. Spectrum: buy (managed AI services) → configure (built-in algorithms) → build (custom training + foundation models). Pick the leftmost option that solves the problem.
  • AI service one-liners: Comprehend = understand text · Transcribe = speech→text · Polly = text→speech · Translate = languages · Lex = chatbot · Kendra = search · Rekognition = vision · Textract = documents · Personalize = recommendations · A2I = human-in-the-loop review.
  • Customize a managed model before building one: Comprehend custom classification/entities, Rekognition Custom Labels, Transcribe custom vocabulary.
  • Amazon Q: Q Business (answers over your enterprise data) · Q Developer (AWS-aware coding assistant, CodeWhisperer's successor) · Q Apps (prompt-to-app).
  • AI hardware: Trainium = training, Inferentia = inference, both via the Neuron SDK, both chosen for price-performance vs GPUs.
  • Input modes: File (download all) · Pipe (stream from S3) · FastFile (stream, accessed as local). Dataset too big for the volume → Pipe or FastFile.
  • Built-in heavy hitters: XGBoost (tabular), DeepAR (forecasting), Random Cut Forest (anomaly), K-Means (cluster), PCA (dim-reduction), BlazingText (text), Object Detection / Image Classification (vision), IP Insights (security), Factorization Machines (sparse/recs), Seq2Seq (translation/summarization), LDA & Neural Topic Model (topics).
  • Algorithm selection logic: labeled? → supervised vs unsupervised → match the problem shape (tabular / time-series / text / vision / anomaly).
  • Human review, two stages: Ground Truth labels data before training; A2I reviews predictions after deployment.
  • The two traps: over-engineering (custom model when an API works) and under-powering (generic API on a domain-specific problem).
