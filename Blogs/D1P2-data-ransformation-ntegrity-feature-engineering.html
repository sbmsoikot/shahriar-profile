Domain 1 is 28% of the MLA-C01 — the largest slice — and it splits into three task statements. Part 1 covered Task 1.1 (ingest & store). This post covers the other two: Task 1.2 (transform data & perform feature engineering) and Task 1.3 (ensure data integrity & prepare data for modeling).

Previously on MetroPulse In Part 1 we designed the ingestion & storage layer for MetroPulse — a city-scale mobility platform (e-scooters, e-bikes, shuttle vans). Vehicle telemetry and app events stream through Kinesis Data Streams; Managed Service for Apache Flink scores anomalies in real time; Amazon Data Firehose lands everything as Parquet in an S3 data lake organized into Bronze (raw) → Silver (clean) → Gold (curated) zones, catalogued by AWS Glue. That gave us durable, queryable, secured data. But raw events are not features. This post is where the data actually becomes usable for ML.

One quick scope note before we build. This part of Domain 1 threads through a lot of services — EMR and Spark, the feature-engineering fundamentals, the whole SageMaker AI data-prep suite, Glue, and Athena. Rather than tour them one at a time, I'll let the MetroPulse build pull each one in exactly where a real engineer would reach for it. Two AWS naming updates are already baked in below, because they will trip you up on the exam if you learned the old names: on December 3, 2024 the original Amazon SageMaker was renamed SageMaker AI (it's now one component inside the next-generation, unified Amazon SageMaker), and SageMaker Data Wrangler no longer has its own console — it lives inside SageMaker Canvas.

01 / THE HANDOFFWhat Part 2 has to accomplish — and a small confession about the project

Before writing this post I went back and stress-tested the MetroPulse scenario against everything Task 1.2 and 1.3 expect you to know. The verdict: the project holds up, but it needed enriching, not replacing. Here's the honest reasoning, because it's the same judgment call you'll make on real systems.

Part 1's MetroPulse was almost entirely numeric and time-series data: GPS coordinates, battery levels, ride durations, payment amounts. That's perfect for teaching imputation, outliers, scaling, and encoding. But Section 3 also teaches text feature engineering (TF-IDF) and data labeling (Ground Truth, Mechanical Turk) — and you can't motivate those honestly with telemetry alone. So rather than bolt on contrived examples, I added two data facets that any real mobility company genuinely has:

The brief · Part 2 (two new data facets)

Same MetroPulse, richer data

Everything from Part 1 stays exactly as it was. We're adding two sources that were always implied by the business:

  • Rider support tickets & in-app feedback — free text. "App charged me twice." "Scooter #4471 brakes feel loose." This unlocks TF-IDF, text vectorization, and PII handling — and feeds a future ticket-routing model.
  • Field-reported damage photos — images uploaded by ops staff and riders. Unlabeled, they're worthless; labeled (bounding boxes around cracks, bent forks), they train a damage-triage model. This is the canonical job for Ground Truth + a human workforce.

The mission for this post: take the raw Bronze-zone lake plus these two new facets, and produce clean, validated, feature-rich datasets that Part 3's models can train on — without leaking PII, without bias we didn't check for, and at a scale that won't fall over.

Exam lens The exam loves the seam between 1.2 and 1.3. 1.2 is "make features" (transform, encode, scale, reduce dimensionality, engineer signal). 1.3 is "make sure the data is trustworthy and ready" (validate quality, fix imbalance, detect bias and PII, split and shuffle correctly). When a question describes a problem with the data, it's usually testing 1.3; when it describes creating model inputs, it's 1.2.

02 / SCALEWhen the data is too big for one machine: EMR & Spark

MetroPulse lands billions of telemetry rows a month. Our first transformation job — sessionize raw GPS pings into discrete rides, then compute per-ride features like distance, average speed, and battery drain rate — simply will not run on a single instance. This is where distributed processing earns its keep.

The lineage matters for the exam. Apache Hadoop introduced the idea of splitting work across a cluster (HDFS for storage, MapReduce for compute). Apache Spark kept the distributed model but moved the working set in-memory and replaced rigid map-reduce stages with a flexible DAG of transformations — making it dramatically faster for the iterative, multi-step pipelines that feature engineering actually is. Amazon EMR (Elastic MapReduce) is the managed home for this whole ecosystem: Spark, Hive, Presto/Trino, HBase, Flink and more, with AWS handling provisioning, scaling, and patching.

Choosing your EMR flavor

EMR comes in three deployment models, and the exam expects you to match them to a scenario:

FlavorYou manageReach for it when…
EMR on EC2Cluster sizing, instance fleets, scaling policiesLong-running clusters, full control, custom bootstrap, spot-instance cost tuning
EMR ServerlessNothing — just submit the jobSpiky or unpredictable workloads; you want per-job autoscaling and to pay only for what runs
EMR on EKSShared Kubernetes capacityYou already standardize on EKS and want Spark to share that infrastructure

For MetroPulse, the nightly ride-sessionization batch is steady, so EMR Serverless is the low-ops default; if we later add an always-on interactive analytics cluster for the data-science team, EMR on EC2 with spot instances becomes attractive. Notebooks and job authoring happen in EMR Studio.

Common trap EMR vs Glue is the perennial exam fork. Both run managed Spark. Choose AWS Glue when you want serverless ETL tightly integrated with the Data Catalog and minimal infrastructure thinking. Choose EMR when you need the broader Hadoop ecosystem (Hive, Presto, HBase, custom Spark versions), fine-grained cluster/cost control, or very large, long-running clusters. "Managed Spark with the least operational overhead and Catalog integration" → Glue. "Open-source big-data ecosystem with control" → EMR.

03 / SIGNALFeature engineering & the curse of dimensionality

A feature is any measurable input the model learns from. Feature engineering is the craft of turning raw columns into features that actually carry signal — and it is, hands down, the highest-leverage work in classical ML. A mediocre algorithm on great features beats a great algorithm on raw junk almost every time. For MetroPulse, "timestamp" is raw; hour-of-day, is-weekend, minutes-since-last-ride are features.

But more features is not better. As you add dimensions, the volume of the feature space grows exponentially, and your fixed number of training rows spreads thinner and thinner across it. Points that should be "neighbors" drift apart, distance-based methods lose meaning, and models overfit the emptiness. That's the curse of dimensionality.

The defenses fall into two buckets. Feature selection keeps a subset of the original features (filter methods by correlation, wrapper methods like recursive elimination, or embedded methods like L1 regularization). Dimensionality reduction creates new, fewer features that preserve most of the variance — PCA being the workhorse (a SageMaker built-in algorithm we'll meet in Part 3). The practical rule for MetroPulse: don't one-hot-encode a 500-value categorical column without asking whether grouping, target encoding, or an embedding would carry the same signal in far fewer dimensions.

Exam lens If a scenario describes a model that trains fine but generalizes terribly, with "hundreds of features" and "not that many rows," the curse of dimensionality is the intended diagnosis — and PCA / feature selection is the intended fix. Pair the symptom (sparse, high-dimensional, overfitting) with the remedy.

04 / THE GAUNTLETCleaning & transforming: the feature engineering gauntlet

Every raw dataset has to run a gauntlet before a model should ever see it. These are the bread-and-butter techniques the exam tests directly, and they apply whether you implement them in Spark, Glue, or SageMaker Data Wrangler. Here's the MetroPulse run through each station.

Missing data

MetroPulse scooters in parking-garage dead zones drop GPS for a few pings. Options, in rough order of sophistication: drop rows/columns (fine if missingness is tiny and random; dangerous otherwise), simple imputation (mean/median for numerics, mode for categoricals — median resists outliers), or model-based imputation (KNN or regression imputation that predicts the missing value from the other columns). The why behind the missingness matters: data missing completely at random is safe to impute simply; data missing because of the thing you're predicting needs care or it bakes in bias.

Outliers

A scooter reporting 300 km/h is a sensor glitch, not a feature. Detect with the Z-score (how many standard deviations from the mean) or the IQR method (points beyond 1.5× the interquartile range). Then decide: cap/winsorize to a sane bound, remove, or transform (a log transform tames long right tails). Don't reflexively delete — sometimes the outlier is the fraud you're trying to catch.

Imbalanced classes

Payment fraud is maybe 0.3% of rides. Train naively and the model hits 99.7% accuracy by predicting "never fraud" — useless. Fixes: oversample the minority (including synthetic samples via SMOTE), undersample the majority, apply class weights so errors on the rare class cost more, or generate more data. And critically, judge the model on precision/recall/AUC, not raw accuracy.

Encoding categoricals

Models eat numbers, not strings. One-hot encoding turns vehicle_type ∈ {scooter, bike, van} into three 0/1 columns — great for low-cardinality, no false ordering. Label/ordinal encoding maps categories to integers — only valid when there's a real order (e.g. small < medium < large), otherwise the model invents a ranking that isn't there. High-cardinality columns (like station_id) are where one-hot explodes dimensionality — reach for grouping, target encoding, or embeddings.

Scaling numerics

Battery percentage (0–100) and ride distance (0–40000 m) live on wildly different scales; distance- and gradient-based models will let the big-magnitude feature dominate. Standardization (z-score: mean 0, std 1) suits roughly Gaussian features and is the common default; normalization (min-max to [0,1]) suits bounded features. Tree-based models don't need scaling; most everything else does.

Binning, transforms & shuffling

Binning buckets a continuous feature into ranges (turn exact age into age-bands) to reduce noise and capture non-linear effects. Log/power transforms reshape skewed distributions. Shuffling randomizes row order so training batches aren't accidentally sorted by time or label — essential when your raw data arrived in chronological order, as MetroPulse's always does.

The #1 leakage trap Fit your scalers, imputers, and encoders on the training set only, then apply those fitted parameters to validation/test. Computing the mean for standardization over the whole dataset — before splitting — leaks test information into training and inflates your scores. Same rule for SMOTE: oversample after the split, on the training fold only.

05 / TEXTTurning support tickets into features: TF-IDF

Our new ticket stream is free text: "App charged me twice for one ride." To route tickets automatically, a model needs numbers, not sentences. The classic, exam-relevant technique is TF-IDF — and it's a perfect Spark-on-EMR job because we have a lot of tickets.

The pipeline is always the same: tokenize (split into words), drop stop words ("the", "a"), then weight each term. Term Frequency (TF) counts how often a word appears in one ticket. Inverse Document Frequency (IDF) downweights words that appear in every ticket (uninformative) and boosts rare, distinctive ones. Multiply them: "refund" and "brakes" get high TF-IDF scores and become strong routing signals, while "the" and "ride" fade to near zero. Each ticket becomes a sparse numeric vector — exactly what a classifier wants.

// TF-IDF on MetroPulse tickets — PySpark on EMR Serverless

from pyspark.ml.feature import Tokenizer, StopWordsRemover, HashingTF, IDF

# tickets_df has a "text" column of raw ticket bodies
tokens   = Tokenizer(inputCol="text", outputCol="words").transform(tickets_df)
cleaned  = StopWordsRemover(inputCol="words", outputCol="kept").transform(tokens)

# term frequency, then inverse document frequency
tf       = HashingTF(inputCol="kept", outputCol="tf", numFeatures=4096).transform(cleaned)
idf_model = IDF(inputCol="tf", outputCol="features").fit(tf)
features = idf_model.transform(tf)   # -> sparse TF-IDF vector per ticket
Exam lens Know TF-IDF as the bag-of-words baseline for text features, and know its limitation: it captures word importance but not meaning or word order — "not broken" and "broken" look almost identical. The modern alternative is embeddings (dense vectors that capture semantics), which we'll reach for with Amazon Bedrock and Titan/Comprehend in Part 4. If a question stresses semantic similarity or context, the answer is embeddings, not TF-IDF.

06 / LABELSLabeling the damage photos: Ground Truth & Mechanical Turk

Supervised learning needs labeled examples, and our field-reported damage photos arrive with none. To train a damage-triage model, a human has to draw a bounding box around each crack or bent fork. Amazon SageMaker Ground Truth is the managed service that orchestrates exactly this: it presents unlabeled data to a workforce, collects annotations, and produces a clean labeled dataset in S3.

The decision the exam cares about is which workforce. Amazon Mechanical Turk is a public, on-demand crowd — cheapest and most scalable, but you should never send it sensitive or private data. A private workforce (your own employees) is the right call when photos might contain license plates, faces, or anything confidential. A vendor workforce from AWS Marketplace fits when labeling needs specialized expertise. Ground Truth's automated data labeling (active learning) trains a model on the human-labeled examples and uses it to auto-label the confident cases, sending only the ambiguous ones to humans — meaningfully cutting cost on large datasets.

Worth knowing Mechanical Turk is a general crowdsourcing marketplace (work is posted as "HITs") and can be used directly, not only through Ground Truth. And for teams that want labeling fully managed end-to-end, Ground Truth Plus provides a turnkey, AWS-run labeling operation. For routine in-house labeling, Data Wrangler and Canvas can also handle simpler annotation needs.

07 / THE SUITESageMaker AI & the no/low-code data-prep tools

Spark and Glue are the heavy machinery. But a lot of MetroPulse feature work is faster in SageMaker's visual tools — and this is the area with the most naming churn, so let's pin it down first.

Two renames the exam will test On December 3, 2024, the original Amazon SageMaker was renamed Amazon SageMaker AI. The "next generation" Amazon SageMaker is now a unified platform — SageMaker Unified Studio — that brings EMR, Glue, Athena, Redshift, MWAA, Bedrock, and SageMaker AI into one governed workspace. Existing API namespaces (the sagemaker SDK) are unchanged for backward compatibility. Second: SageMaker Data Wrangler no longer has a standalone console — it now lives inside SageMaker Canvas. If a question (or a colleague) says "open Data Wrangler," the answer is "it's under Canvas now."

A SageMaker AI Domain is the top-level workspace that ties together users, their storage, and permissions — the boundary your team's notebooks, jobs, and apps live inside. With that vocabulary set, here's the data-prep toolkit and what each is for.

Data Wrangler is the visual workhorse: import from 50+ sources, apply any of 300+ built-in transforms (the exact impute/outlier/encode/scale operations from the gauntlet above), drive it with SQL or a natural-language chat, generate a Data Quality and Insights report that flags missing values and class imbalance automatically, and scale the whole flow onto Spark processing jobs. Canvas wraps it for fully no-code users who also want to build and use models without writing code. Both export cleanly into the rest of the ML lifecycle — most importantly, into the Feature Store.

08 / REUSESageMaker Feature Store: prepare once, serve everywhere

Here's a problem that bites every growing ML team: the demand-forecasting model and the fraud model both need "rides in the last hour per station" — and two engineers compute it two slightly different ways. Now training and production disagree, and nobody can tell why. SageMaker Feature Store solves this by being the single, governed home for engineered features, organized into feature groups.

It has two faces. The online store serves single-digit-millisecond reads for real-time inference — when a ride is booked, the fraud endpoint pulls that rider's current features instantly. The offline store lands the full history in S3 for training and batch jobs. Crucially, Feature Store supports point-in-time (time-travel) retrieval: when building a training set, you fetch each feature's value as it was at the moment of the event — not today's value — which prevents a subtle but deadly form of data leakage.

Exam lens Map the two stores to the two read patterns: online = real-time inference (low latency, latest value), offline = training/batch (full history in S3). And remember Feature Store's headline benefits: feature reuse across teams and eliminating training/serving skew. "How do we stop two teams computing the same feature differently?" → Feature Store.

09 / TRUSTIntegrity, bias & explainability: Clarify, SHAP & Model Monitor

Task 1.3 isn't just "is the data clean" — it's "is the data trustworthy." A demand-forecasting model that quietly under-serves lower-income neighborhoods, or a fraud model nobody can explain to a disputing rider, is a liability. AWS gives us tooling to check this before and after training.

SageMaker Clarify runs bias detection at two moments. Pre-training, it measures whether the data itself is skewed across sensitive groups — for example, whether damage labels or ride coverage are imbalanced by neighborhood. Post-training, it measures whether the model's predictions are skewed. Clarify also powers explainability using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) — a method rooted in the game-theory concept of Shapley values that assigns each feature a contribution to an individual prediction.

The related tool you'll see named is the Partial Dependence Plot (PDP), which shows how predictions change on average as one feature varies across its range — a global view, where SHAP gives the local, per-prediction view. Together they answer "what does the model rely on, overall?" and "why this specific decision?"

Honest scope note SageMaker Model Monitor watches a deployed model for drift — data-quality drift, model-quality drift, bias drift, and feature-attribution drift — by comparing live traffic against a baseline you capture during data prep. That baselining belongs here in Domain 1; the ongoing monitoring is really Domain 4 (Task 4.1), so we'll go deep on it in the MLOps post. I'm flagging it now because the exam sometimes places the baseline creation step in the data-preparation context.

10 / GLUEAWS Glue for transformation: Studio, Data Quality & DataBrew

In Part 1, Glue catalogued our lake. In Part 2 it does the transforming. Three Glue capabilities matter here, and they map neatly to three different users.

AWS Glue Studio is the visual job authoring tool: drag source, transform, and target nodes onto a canvas and Glue generates the underlying serverless Spark job. It's the data engineer's tool for production ETL — promoting MetroPulse telemetry from Bronze to Silver, joining partner feeds, repartitioning to Parquet. AWS Glue DataBrew is the analyst's tool: a fully no-code, point-and-click interface with 250+ transforms and rich data profiling, perfect for letting an ops analyst clean a partner CSV without writing Spark.

The integrity piece is AWS Glue Data Quality. You declare rules in DQDL (Data Quality Definition Language) and Glue evaluates them against Catalog tables or inside ETL jobs — exactly the Task 1.3 work of guaranteeing the data is fit before models depend on it.

// Glue Data Quality — DQDL ruleset guarding the Silver ride table

Rules = [
    # every ride must have an id and a vehicle
    IsComplete "ride_id",
    IsComplete "vehicle_id",
    Uniqueness "ride_id" > 0.99,
    # physically plausible values only
    ColumnValues "avg_speed_kmh" between 0 and 80,
    ColumnValues "battery_pct" between 0 and 100,
    # freshness: data should be recent
    ColumnValues "event_date" > (now() - 2 days)
]

PII: find it and mask it before it spreads

Our support tickets are full of personal data — names, emails, phone numbers, the occasional card fragment. That cannot flow into a TF-IDF model or a shared training set unmasked. AWS gives you layered options: DataBrew PII transforms and Glue's PII detection can detect and then mask, hash, encrypt, or redact sensitive fields during transformation; Amazon Comprehend detects PII entities in free text (its DetectPiiEntities capability is what powers redaction in ticket bodies); and Amazon Macie scans S3 at large to discover where sensitive data is hiding in the first place.

Exam lens Match the PII tool to the surface: Macie = discover sensitive data sitting in S3; Comprehend = detect/redact PII entities in text; Glue/DataBrew PII transforms = mask or hash PII during ETL. "Find PII across the data lake" is Macie; "redact names from support tickets" is Comprehend; "hash emails as part of the prep job" is Glue/DataBrew.

11 / ATHENAAthena: query, materialize & govern the curated layer

The last mile is turning prepared data into clean, fast, governed tables the modeling team can actually query. Amazon Athena is serverless SQL straight over S3, using the Glue Data Catalog as its schema. Beyond ad-hoc queries, it has three features that matter for Task 1.2/1.3.

First, CTAS (CREATE TABLE AS SELECT) materializes a query result as a new table — the idiomatic way to build a curated, partitioned, columnar Gold feature table from messier Silver inputs, in one statement.

// Athena CTAS — build the model-ready Gold feature table

CREATE TABLE metropulse.gold_ride_features
WITH (
    format = 'PARQUET',
    parquet_compression = 'SNAPPY',
    partitioned_by = ARRAY['event_date']
) AS
SELECT ride_id, vehicle_type, hour_of_day, is_weekend,
       avg_speed_kmh, battery_drain_rate, station_demand_1h,
       event_date
FROM   metropulse.silver_rides
WHERE  avg_speed_kmh BETWEEN 0 AND 80;

Second, performance is mostly about scanning less, because Athena bills by data scanned. The big levers: store as columnar Parquet (read only the columns you need), partition by a common filter like event_date, compress, use partition projection to skip slow partition lookups on huge tables, and never SELECT * when you need three columns. These are the same wins that made our Part 1 format choices (Parquet over JSON) pay off.

Third, governance. Athena ACID transactions — backed by Apache Iceberg tables — bring INSERT/UPDATE/DELETE/MERGE and time travel to the lake, so late-arriving fare corrections can be upserted safely instead of rewriting whole partitions. And Athena fine-grained access control via AWS Lake Formation enforces column-, row-, and cell-level permissions — so analysts can query ride features while the PII-bearing columns from our tickets stay locked to authorized roles only.

Lakehouse callback Notice this is the lakehouse from Part 1 made concrete: S3 + Iceberg (ACID, time travel) + Lake Formation (governance) + Athena (SQL) gives warehouse-grade behavior on lake-grade economics — one copy of the data, queried by both BI and ML.

Step back and the whole transformation-and-integrity layer snaps together. Part 1's raw lake and our two new facets flow left to right: get transformed and feature-engineered, get validated and governed, and land as curated tables and a feature store that Part 3's models will train on.

Part 2 revision cheat-sheet

  • Domain 1 = three tasks. 1.1 ingest/store (Part 1); 1.2 transform & feature-engineer; 1.3 ensure integrity & prepare. This post = 1.2 + 1.3.
  • EMR vs Glue: Glue for serverless, Catalog-integrated ETL with least ops; EMR for the Hadoop ecosystem, custom Spark, and cluster control. EMR Serverless for spiky jobs.
  • The gauntlet: impute (mean/median/mode, KNN) → handle outliers (Z-score/IQR, cap or log) → balance (SMOTE/under/over/class weights) → encode (one-hot vs ordinal-only-when-ordered) → scale (standardize vs normalize) → bin/transform/shuffle.
  • The #1 leakage rule: fit scalers/imputers/encoders on the training split only; oversample (SMOTE) after the split.
  • Curse of dimensionality: too many features + too few rows → sparsity & overfitting → fix with feature selection or PCA.
  • TF-IDF = bag-of-words importance weighting; no semantics → use embeddings when meaning/context matters.
  • Labeling workforce: Mechanical Turk = public/scale/non-sensitive; private = sensitive data; vendor = specialist skills. Active learning auto-labels the easy cases.
  • SageMaker renames: SageMaker → SageMaker AI (Dec 3 2024); the unified Amazon SageMaker / Unified Studio is the umbrella; Data Wrangler lives inside Canvas.
  • Feature Store: online = real-time/low-latency; offline = training/history in S3; point-in-time retrieval prevents leakage.
  • Clarify = pre- & post-training bias + SHAP (local, per-prediction) vs PDP (global). Model Monitor baselines here, monitors in Domain 4.
  • PII mapping: Macie (discover in S3) · Comprehend (detect/redact in text) · Glue/DataBrew (mask during ETL).
  • Athena: CTAS to materialize Parquet/partitioned tables; scan less (columnar, partition, compress, partition projection); ACID via Apache Iceberg; fine-grained access via Lake Formation.
